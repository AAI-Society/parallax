\noindent
This records what the implementation settled, what it forced us to correct, and
what P01 needs to say differently as a result. It exists because the working
notes lived in git-ignored scratch, and these conclusions should not.

\vspace{0.6em}

\noindent
\keypoint{This is a snapshot of the trust calculus alone.} The counts on the
cover are the repository as it stood at the twelve-task whole-branch review, and
they are correct for it; they are not current. Work that landed afterwards ---
real Intel TDX quote verification, RA-TLS key binding, collateral fetch and
cache, and \texttt{parallax-proxy} --- is not described here and did not exist
when this was written. For what the tool does now, and for current figures, read
the repository \texttt{README.md} and \texttt{docs/STANDARD-MAP.md}. Nothing
below has been revised in the light of that work.

\section*{Amendments P01 needs}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \keypoint{The termination argument is simpler than the paper claims, and
now has evidence.} P01's mechanization section proposes SLG resolution with
tabling, in the style of XSB, to stop circular attestation from diverging. That
is not what the tool does and not what is needed. Modelling the residual trust
set as a bounded lattice --- join is set union over a finite principal set, so
the powerset has finite height --- makes the least fixpoint terminate by
construction, regardless of cycles in the delegation graph. Termination falls
out of the datatype rather than the resolution strategy.

This has been exercised well past the point of doubt: a 300-node delegation
cycle spanning two mechanism layers terminates in about 0.01\,s, and a 40-node
complete delegation graph with all 1,600 edges including self-loops terminates
in about 1\,ms. \keypoint{Rewrite the mechanization section around the
bounded-lattice argument} and drop the tabling proposal; it is a weaker claim
requiring more machinery.

Note also that only \emph{one} of the two lattices carries termination. The
latency lattice has infinite height ($\mathrm{Bounded}(0) < \mathrm{Bounded}(1)
< \dots$); the fixpoint never iterates in it. Saying ``two lattices of finite
height'' is false, and a formal-methods referee catches it immediately.

\item \keypoint{\S4's independent-encoding experiment needs an
order-independence claim, and the reason is not academic.} P01 offers
independent double-encoding as its answer to the completeness problem: two
authors encode the same deployment and any divergence is a finding about the
calculus. That experiment is worthless unless the comparison is invariant to how
the deployment was written down, and during development it twice was not.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Mechanism identity was initially \emph{positional}
    (\texttt{tee\_attestation\#0}), so a deployment compared as
    \textsc{Incomparable} to a re-ordering of itself. All twelve non-identical
    example pairs returned \textsc{Incomparable}, meaning the acceptance test
    carrying the paper's headline claim would have passed against an
    implementation that returned \textsc{Incomparable} unconditionally.
  \item After that was fixed, identity was still \emph{lexical} in duration
    fields, so \texttt{collateral\_refresh = "12h"} and \texttt{"720m"} ---
    identical in every computed value --- again produced total divergence.
\end{itemize}

Both are fixed and pinned by tests. The paper should state plainly that the
comparison is order-independent and duration-normalised \keypoint{by
construction}, because two independent encoders writing \texttt{12h} and
\texttt{720m} is not a hypothetical.

\item \keypoint{Do not overstate the incomparability result.} The tool ranks
only pairs that establish the same claim; comparing trust sets for different
propositions is refused. Of the twelve ordered pairs across the four non-hybrid
examples, eight are N/A on that ground and four are \textsc{Incomparable} ---
that is two unordered pairs, $\Sigma_1/\Sigma_3$ and $\Sigma_2/\Sigma_4$. The
claim is real but narrower than ``trust sets come out incomparable'' implies.

\item \keypoint{The evidence base is five self-authored deployments and no
real-world system.} The tool reproduces the hand-derived five-party TDX set and
finds the shared build-pipeline dependency in the hybrid, with a negative
control that correctly reports nothing. That is the whole of it. Say so.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{What the implementation confirmed}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \keypoint{The five-party TDX set is derivable from the deployment
    description}, not hardcoded --- verified by collapsing a principal in the
    input and watching the count track it. Exactly one of the five, the
    collateral authority, has a bounded detection latency; the other four are
    silent and permanent. That asymmetry is the paper's central empirical claim
    and it holds.
  \item \keypoint{$\Sigma_2$ and $\Sigma_4$ are genuinely incomparable} --- five
    disjoint assumptions on each side, same claim, no artifact of the encoding.
  \item \keypoint{The hybrid's two layers do share a build pipeline}, and the
    independent-pipelines variant reports nothing. The finding is discovery, not
    theatre.
  \item \keypoint{Anchoring is a trade, not a pure gain.} Anchoring to a
    settlement layer converts log-operator trust into a bounded detection window
    and silently ingests a consensus-execution-fidelity assumption. Both appear
    in the manifest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{The defect pattern worth carrying to \texttt{transit} and \texttt{occultation}}

Every Important-or-worse finding across twelve tasks and the final review was a
defect in the \keypoint{plan}, not in an implementation. Five were Critical, and
all five shared a shape: \keypoint{correct code implementing a subtly wrong
specification, producing a confident wrong answer rather than an error.}

\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{6.2cm}p{8.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Defect} & \textbf{Wrong answer it produced} \\
\midrule
Positional mechanism tag &
  a file \textsc{Incomparable} to a reordering of itself \\
Lexical duration identity &
  \texttt{12h} vs \texttt{720m} reported as total divergence \\
Flat \texttt{"delegation"} tag &
  a single-layer deployment reporting shared dependencies \\
\texttt{via\_delegation} per principal &
  a dead-end delegate named as a reason a principal matters \\
\texttt{Policy} without \texttt{deny\_unknown\_fields} &
  a one-letter typo silently disabling the gate; \texttt{check} exits 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.4em}

Four of the five trace to one root cause: \keypoint{a field participating in a
value's identity was specified casually.} Anything that feeds
\texttt{Eq}/\texttt{Ord}/\texttt{Hash} is load-bearing for every comparison
downstream, and specifying it loosely produces answers that look right.

The two checks that would have caught all five, and which belong in any plan of
this kind:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \keypoint{For every derived identity, test that semantically identical
    inputs compare equal} --- reordered, respelled, re-serialised.
  \item \keypoint{For every configuration surface, reject unknown keys.} A
    silently ignored typo in a security policy is a gate that fails open.
\end{enumerate}

The reviewers earned their cost by going to primary sources rather than trusting
the plan's paraphrase --- reading \texttt{ascent\_base}'s \texttt{Lattice} trait
instead of the brief's summary of it, rebuilding pre-fix binaries to confirm a
repro, and mutation-testing claims that tests were supposed to pin. That last
technique found the delegation-direction gap that five passing tests missed.

\section*{Carried, not fixed}

None affects a claim the paper makes.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Table 2 in the paper is pinned to the code by a tripwire test covering
    mechanism, capability, latency and impact --- but nothing pins the caption's
    prose to the table. Three separate factual errors have been found in that
    caption; generating the table from source is the real fix.
  \item The CI \texttt{paper} job has never run; \texttt{setup-tectonic} on
    Ubuntu is unverified.
  \item \texttt{shared\_dependencies(d, t)} requires \texttt{t == solve(d)} by
    convention, not by type. A \texttt{Solved} newtype would remove this and
    \texttt{explain}'s vestigial parameter together.
  \item \texttt{policy::evaluate} does not itself verify \texttt{\$schema}; the
    CLI does.
\end{itemize}
